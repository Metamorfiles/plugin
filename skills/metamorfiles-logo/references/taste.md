# Logo taste

What a strong logo looks like, from a study of current identity work across food, drink, fashion,
sport, culture, services and tech. It describes; it never decides. The brief and the chosen direction
decide the look. This is how to tell a strong version of that look from a weak one, and what the
image model gets wrong on the way. A character has its own page: `references/taste.md` of
`metamorfiles-character`.

## The point of view

- **One idea, one hand.** A logo is one idea drawn by one hand: the letters, the mark, the character
  and every small word look made with the same tool on the same day.
- **The name comes first.** Most strong logos are the name with one thing changed. A mark earns its
  place with a reason from the business, not from the category.
- **Boldness in one place.** One loud part (the letters, the character, the swapped letter) held by
  quiet, exact partners (a plain line of small capitals, a thin ring).
- **Character is a choice, not noise.** Uneven widths, a bounce, a cut edge are drawn on purpose and
  repeated with intent. Accidents look like accidents.
- **Made for its world.** It already looks like it belongs on this business's cup, box, sign, apron
  or app icon, at the size it meets there.
- **A family, not a file.** A strong logo comes as a small set: the main lockup, a one-line version,
  the mark alone, often a seal or tag. Each is the same parts rearranged, never redrawn.

## Lettering families

Each is a direction the brand may choose. What makes it strong, and where generated versions fall
short.

**Chunky and soft.** Heavy letters with rounded corners, tight enough to nearly touch, small
counters.
- Strong: each letter has one quirk of its own (a slot counter, a ball end, a flat foot, a lowercase
  letter among capitals), and the word reads as one soft mass.
- Weak: a stock bubble face with nothing drawn, every letter the same width.

**Cut and hand-cut.** Letters that look cut from paper or card: straight cuts, faceted corners, uneven
widths and slants, a little lean.
- Strong: the cut is consistent (same scissors, same directness), and the mark and figures are cut
  by the same hand.
- Weak: random jitter, curves sneaking in, letters that wobble without a cut's logic.

**Casual script and brush.** Joined letters written fast (brush, marker or rounded monoline), a rising
baseline, one long gesture (a tail, an underline, a swash off the last letter).
- Strong: one generous gesture and everything else calm; joins a hand would make.
- Weak: swashes everywhere, joins across words, letters that change pen halfway.

**Monoline and signature.** One even line, like a single pen stroke: loose loops, open curves, wide
spacing, a double letter drawn as one gesture.
- Strong: the line never changes weight; the small words beside it are light, widely spaced
  capitals.
- Weak: the line thickens where it crosses, loops fill in.

**Formal script.** High-contrast pointed-pen or brush strokes, a big capital, one long flourish.
- Strong: the flourish frames something (a line tucked under it, a number).
- Weak: flourishes on every letter, a wedding-invitation look.

**Retro display.** 70s soft serifs and slabs, flared stems, melting or wavy letters, sign-painter
italics.
- Strong: the period is felt in the shapes, not imitated with effects.
- Weak: drop shadows and textures standing in for drawing.

**Classic serif.** Calm display capitals, wedge serifs, even, open spacing, small capitals beside
them.
- Strong: restraint, generous space, one fine detail (a diamond dot, a ligature).
- Weak: a text serif set tight and large with nothing of its own.

**Grotesque and geometric.** Plain sans letters: heavy and wide for a badge system, medium and tight
for a modern company.
- Strong: one letter redrawn to carry the idea (a pulse for an m, a cut in a corner), or a strict
  system around a plain word.
- Weak: a default sans beside a generic symbol.

**Condensed hand-drawn.** Tall, narrow capitals with a slight flare and an uneven hand, for heritage
and vintage.
- Strong: rhythm and evenness from a practised hand.
- Weak: a stock condensed face pretending.

## Composition moves

The ways the parts meet, chosen by the brand's world and where the logo will live:
- **The name alone,** with its one anomaly.
- **A letter swapped for an object that still reads as the letter**: a cookie for an O, a fruit for a
  dot, a citrus slice for an accent. The object takes the letters' weight and colour, its details cut
  out as holes.
- **A letter that becomes the mark:** a G that is a face, an m inside a pebble, initials packed into
  a shield.
- **The mark in the stack's empty corner:** two lines of different lengths, the mark filling the gap.
- **The character above the name,** behind it and overlapping, or peeking over it.
- **The name arched over the character or a crest,** a line of small type below.
- **Small words at the name's ends,** justified to its width (the trade on the left, the year on the
  right).
- **A line tucked under a script,** from under its first letters to under its last.
- **Words around a ring,** the first phrase centred at the top, the bottom phrase either following
  the ring or turned to read upright. Both are used well; the brand's register decides.
- **A badge family:** ring, shield, pill, arch, tab and box, each in the same type and line weight.
- **The name in a shape:** a square, a sticker outline, a scalloped frame.

Strong: the parts share one line weight, the space between them is deliberate, and it stays the same
in every file. Weak: everything centred and the same size, parts floating with no relation, a lockup
that changes from file to file.

## Marks

- **Same hand as the letters.** A cut-paper name gets a cut-paper mark; a monoline script gets a
  monoline mark. The hand is the line weight, the edges and the corners; a character keeps its face
  and its drawing, never turned into a silhouette to match a heavy face.
- **One idea, simple enough to cut out,** and still itself at 32 px in one colour.
- **From the business, not the category.** Left alone, the image model reaches for the category's
  symbol (a mountain for climbing, wheat for a bakery, a lotus for wellness, a bean for a café) and
  adds it even when nobody asked. It belongs in the logo only when the direction chose it.
- **Copied, never redrawn.** The mark alone is the mark from the logo, the same shape. Drawn again on
  its own it drifts, so it's made from the logo's own file.

## Colour in the logo

One or two inks are the norm: black on a colour, one colour on paper, cream on a deep tone. A third
colour does one job (a comb, a leaf). Counters and cut-outs show the ground; a model left alone tends
to fill them with white, so say it in the prompt.

## Defaults to recognise

What a generator produces when nothing was decided. They aren't forbidden; they're used when the
direction chose them, and recognised when it didn't:
- a stock bubble, geometric or script face set untouched as the "custom" lettering;
- the category's symbol added to every route;
- everything centred, one size, even gaps;
- a badge or a frame around a name that didn't need one;
- gradients, shadows, glows or textures on the logo itself;
- three routes that are one idea in three fonts.

## Three routes, three answers

The three routes are three answers, not three versions: each from a different lettering family or
composition move, each holding the direction, each strong in its own terms. The user should be
choosing between ideas.
