# John Lewis Partnership (John Lewis & Partners, Waitrose & Partners)

- **Name / business / place / year / studio:** The John Lewis Partnership, the UK's largest employee-owned business, parent of the department store John Lewis and the supermarket Waitrose (plus Peter Jones, Little Waitrose). UK. Launched September 2018, after roughly three years of work. Pentagram London (partners Harry Pearce, Naresh Ramchandani, William Russell; strategist Carrie Stokes): brand identity, naming ("& Partners"), verbal identity, packaging, digital. ## Brief & business truth
Founded as a trust by John Spedan Lewis in 1929, every employee is a Partner with a share of profit, knowledge and power, so every customer is served by an owner. Two very different retail brands (a department store and a supermarket) plus the parent had drifted apart visually. The brief was to support both retailers commercially, unify the three, work from phone to lorry to store, and make the ownership model visible, a genuine differentiator when consumers want principled brands.

## Strategy / insight
The Partners are the brand's truth; put them into the name itself ("& Partners") and give them a dedicated colour. Use the Partnership's own pattern-making heritage (a haberdasher founder; the Hans Schleger/Peter Hatch identity era of the 1960s–70s) as the springboard for a new system, inspired by, not copying, the past.

## The idea (one sentence) and its source
The Partners are written into every name, and a line pattern from the Partnership's own 1960s archive becomes a single flexible logo-and-pattern system shared by the whole family. **Source:** people (the employee-owners), carried by heritage (the Peter Hatch pattern).

## Register
Editorial heritage modernism: monochrome, Gill capitals, bold stripes, restrained colour.

## Category codes it used or broke
- Broke: supermarket and department-store shouting (price bursts, busy colour), and the anonymous corporate parent brand.
- Used: British heritage typography (Gill), department-store elegance (black and white, spaced capitals), supermarket green for Waitrose, stripes as a retail/haberdashery staple.

## Mark
- **Stacked wordmarks:** JOHN / LEWIS on two lines with "& PARTNERS" beneath at small size, all in widely spaced Gill Sans capitals, redrawn for scale. WAITROSE on one line with "& PARTNERS" beneath. The Partnership's lockup puts JOHN LEWIS / PARTNERSHIP over the two brand lockups.
- **Brandlines logotype (John Lewis):** the stacked wordmark reversed out of a solid black square, preceded on the left by three vertical bars of the same height as the square. Measured from Pentagram's image: bar widths in the ratio 1 : 2 : 4, separated by gaps that shrink 4 : 3 : 1 units, so the bars accelerate into the square; the square is about 27 units wide. The proportions derive from the Peter Hatch pattern (the studio's statement; exact ratios here are measured, inferred).
- **Variants:** full brandlines logo; square alone; wordmark alone; Waitrose's version with horizontal stripe bands in greens below a green panel; parent lockup in Partnership green. Different variants for different applications, "bold in some contexts, quiet in others".

## Wordmark / lettering
Gill Sans capitals (the brands' long-standing face, kept and redrawn), wide letterspacing, centred. "& PARTNERS" set at about a third of the name's cap height, same tracking.

## Type system
- Gill Sans (redrawn cuts): wordmarks, headlines, navigation, product naming.
- Brandlines alphabet: a hand-drawn headline display font based on an old version of Gill, each letter built from parallel inline lines (three strokes per stem), so type carries the pattern.
- Type-as-image: huge Gill capitals cropped off the edges of bags ("JO / HN / LEW / IS" broken across a tote), typographically confident campaign layouts.

## Colour system
- John Lewis: monochrome (black, white, greys) so product and content take centre stage.
- Waitrose: a rich palette of greens from dark to lime, flexible for the louder supermarket context; product-specific colour stripes on jute bags (e.g. red and yellow for bacon and eggs, purple for pancakes).
- Partner Green: a new accent, from the green ink Spedan Lewis used to sign letters; reserved only for Partner activity and communication, applied sparingly (a bag edge), never for decoration. Partnership parent lockups sit on a deep green, about #01584F (sampled, inferred). Waitrose mid green about #5A7E14 (sampled, inferred).

## Device(s) / graphic language
1. **Brandlines pattern:** vertical (John Lewis) or horizontal (Waitrose) bands of varying width in precise proportions; used as livery on vans and lorries, bag patterns, signage edges, carrier handles. It frames, highlights text and imagery, makes shapes, and animates.
2. **Softened lines:** in film and photography, the pattern can be blurred and coloured to sit inside product imagery with depth.
3. **Stripe coding (Waitrose):** stripe sets take colours from the product or recipe named on the bag.
4. **Partner Green as a signal:** a colour with one meaning.

## Imagery
Product-first still life and fashion on plain grounds, strong colour in the product only (John Lewis's black frame makes a red coat or green knit sing); Waitrose food photography dense and natural (veg, dishes). Art direction by Between Us. No lifestyle clutter (inferred).

## Voice
Warm, plain and proud of ownership; the name change is the key verbal move. Naming conventions: every retail brand ends "& Partners"; Waitrose sub-brands like Little Waitrose & Partners; product bag names are simple pairs ("Bacon & Eggs", "Watermelon & Feta"), echoing the "&".

## Applications
Store signage (projecting and fascia), lorries and vans, carrier bags and totes, jute bags, swing tags, labels, apps and web, catalogues, magazines (Waitrose Food, Health), aprons and uniforms, pylon signs, Peter Jones bags, stationery.
- **Fixed:** Gill capitals, stacked wordmark with "& Partners", brandlines proportions, Partner Green meaning.
- **Varies:** orientation of lines (vertical John Lewis, horizontal Waitrose), palette (monochrome vs greens), logo variant by context, pattern density and blur.

## Guidelines structure
Not published. The case study shows the system parts: wordmarks, brandlines logo, pattern, alphabet, colour palettes, Partner Green, applications.

## What makes it ownable
The accelerating bars into a black square; spaced Gill capitals with "& Partners"; the inline brandlines alphabet; monochrome for John Lewis vs greens for Waitrose; Partner Green's single meaning.

## What a generic version would have been
A new modern sans wordmark with a smile or swoosh, one corporate colour across both retailers, stock "happy staff" photography to show ownership, and campaign taglines about caring.

## Principles it demonstrates
- Put the organisation's structural truth into the name rather than into a tagline.
- Mine the archive for a pattern and derive new proportions from it rather than copying it.
- Build one system with two dialects (vertical/monochrome, horizontal/green) so siblings differ yet belong.
- Give one colour one meaning and ration it; scarcity makes it read as a signal.
- Keep the brand neutral so the product supplies the colour in retail.
- Make the logo and the pattern the same object at different scales.
