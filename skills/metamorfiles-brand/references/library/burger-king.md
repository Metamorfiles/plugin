## Brief & business truth

BK had changed its food (removed colours, flavours and preservatives from artificial sources), invested in digital ordering and sustainability, but still looked like its 1999 identity: a tilted, glossy, 3D bun wrapped in a blue swoosh, which JKR likened to a gas-station plastic look. The job: make the brand feel real, craveable and less synthetic; be digital-first (every asset recognisable alone); close the gap between negative fast-food perceptions and the improved food. JKR reports a 7.8% stock rise on launch and a large preference gain over McDonald's.

## Strategy / insight

Everything is derived from the food. JKR explored many territories but kept returning to the 1969-1998 marks, from when the brand "looked at its best" (Lisa Smith). Heritage is the credible route to "real" because it predates the artificial-looking era; food is the source of every asset (colour, type shapes, photography, illustration).

## The idea

**One sentence:** Make the brand look like the food: an honest flat bun-and-name mark from its own past, a typeface you want to bite, and colours named after ingredients.
**Source:** product (the flame-grilled burger and its ingredients), reinforced by the brand's own heritage.

## Register

Warm retro appetite / loud display, flat and chunky; mid-century flavour with contemporary cleanliness.

## Category codes it used or broke

- Broke: QSR gloss (3D bevels, swooshes, speed-lines, blue as a "tech" accent). Blue removed because it is not in food and symbolised artificial ingredients.
- Broke: the corporate practice of a neutral sans for menus. Here the menu type itself is appetising.
- Used: red/orange/yellow appetite colours, but re-grounded in named foods and a warm off-white instead of pure white; brown replaces black.

## Mark

- **Type:** emblem/symbol with integrated wordmark (the "bun halves").
- **Construction (from the official SVG):** two stacked lines of caps, BURGER over KING, both lines justified to the same width; KING is roughly 1.5x the cap height of BURGER. Top bun: a dome (half-ellipse) with a flat base and rounded lower corners; bottom bun: a shallow crescent (flat top, curved bottom), noticeably thinner than the top bun. Bun halves in Flaming Orange, letters in Fiery Red, all inside a Mayo Egg White outer silhouette that follows the burger shape with an even margin (a "keyline" plate, used on colour grounds).
- **Letterforms:** extremely heavy, rounded; stroke ends fully rounded; counters reduced to small rounded slots (B, R); deep V-notches (ink traps) where strokes meet in K, R and N; the G's spur curls inward like a swirl. Compared with 1969/1994: bun shapes and proportions adjusted to look more like a real burger, type streamlined and rounded.
- **Variants:** full-colour emblem; the emblem without the cream plate on cream grounds; single-colour; BK monogram/favicon in which the mark's shapes hide a B and K (described by JKR as an easter egg).

## Wordmark / lettering

Custom lettering in the emblem (not a font); closely related to the Flame family. All caps, tight spacing, two lines, justified width.

## Type system

- **Flame / Flame Bold** (Colophon Foundry, custom, owned by BK): display; described as soft serif, shapes inspired by the food (rounded, bold, "yummy"). Flame Bold has a variable version used stretched and compressed as illustration (e.g. "MMMMMMMM" growing along a billboard, "JUICY" set vertically). Ligatures and swashes for packaging.
- **Flame Sans:** flared sans for menu boards, packaging info, digital UI, legal.
- **Type-as-image moves:** product names set in Flame Bold warped into the round shape of the wrapped burger ("Cheesy Bacon", "Melty Juicy", "Plant Based", "Tender Crispy") so each wrapper is identified by lettering and colour alone; headlines stacked and scaled to fill panels.

## Colour system

Primary (equity colours): Fiery Red #D62300 (wordmark, headlines), Flaming Orange #FF8732 (bun halves, packaging grounds), BBQ Brown #502314 (uniforms, interiors, dark text; replaces black). Secondary: Mayo Egg White #F5EBDC (backgrounds, the plate behind the mark), Melty Yellow #FFAA00 (accent, illustration), Crunchy Green #198737 (freshness, plant-based). Hexes via Canny Creative's summary of BK guidelines (third-party). Rules: no blue; warm off-white rather than pure white; colours named after food (published by BK/It's Nice That).

## Device(s) / graphic language

- **Ingredient illustration** (Cachete Jack): flat, bold, irreverent shapes of tomatoes, lettuce, pickles, onions, flame rings and hands holding burgers; limited palette; big crops that bleed off wrappers and walls; faces and hands with cheeky gestures.
- **Stretchable Flame Bold:** type compressed or expanded to fit a surface, becoming the image itself.
- **Flat mark as sticker:** the emblem sits small and flat, like a stamp, in a corner of busy compositions (observed).

## Imagery

Hyper-textured macro food photography: extreme close-up cross-sections of the Whopper (sesame, lettuce, tomato slices, pickles, charred patty), sharp, saturated but real, dialling up the sensory. Never: glossy composited "perfect" burgers from far away, synthetic blue lighting (inferred from rationale).

## Voice

Playful, irreverent, bold, sensory; words chosen to trigger appetite ("Freshly sliced", "Juicy", "Flame grilling since 1954" on aprons, "Dive in"). The brand's long-running irreverent advertising tone is kept; design supplies the appetite (inferred).

## Applications

Burger wrappers, paper bags, cups, fries cartons, trays and tray liners, uniforms (BBQ Brown aprons and shirts with orange/red stripes), menu boards, restaurant signage and interiors, murals, billboards, social and app/web, favicon, merchandise.
**What varies:** wrapper colour and lettering per product; illustration scenes; type stretching per format. **What stays fixed:** the bun-halves mark, the six food colours, the Flame family, macro-food photography.

## Guidelines structure

Internal guidelines exist (Canny cites BK's own identity guidelines for colour values) but are not public. Observed system parts: logo and monogram, colour (primary/secondary), typography (Flame, Flame Bold variable, Flame Sans), illustration, photography, packaging architecture, uniforms, environments.

## What makes it ownable

The flat bun-halves emblem; Flame Bold's round, ink-trapped shapes; product names bent into the burger shape; BBQ Brown as the dark colour; the food-named palette.

## What a generic version would have been

A refreshed 1999 mark: glossier 3D bun, a new swoosh, a geometric sans for all copy, bright primary red-yellow-blue, stock "hero burger" photography.

## Principles it demonstrates

1. Derive every asset (colour, type shapes, photography, illustration) from the product itself, and name colours after it.
2. Remove any colour or effect that contradicts the product truth, even if it has equity.
3. Before inventing, look in the brand's own archive for the moment it looked most itself, then refine proportions for today.
4. Make the typeface do illustration: letters that can be stretched and shaped to fill a surface replace generic imagery.
5. Design each asset to be recognisable alone (logo, colour, type, photo) for a digital world of fragments.
