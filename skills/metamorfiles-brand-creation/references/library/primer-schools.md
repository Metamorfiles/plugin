# Primer — School for What's Ahead

## Name / business / place / year / studio

- **Name:** Primer (Primer Schools)
- **Business:** a network of small, teacher-led K–8 private schools (microschools of roughly 50–120 students per campus), founded by Ryan Delk. Teachers are called "Primer Leaders".
- **Place:** United States. Started in Florida (Miami area); 14 campuses across Florida, Alabama and Arizona by mid-2026, with Texas (San Antonio) next.
- **Year:** 2026. Launched early June 2026 (Visuelle dated 2 June 2026, The Brand Identity 9 June 2026, Fonts In Use 30 June 2026). newkid's project number is NK-0031.
- **Studio:** newkid (Toronto; Matthew Donne and Rich Brown, co-founders and creative directors; Alex Avendaño, co-founder and strategy director; Clea Forkert, design director). Photography by Luis Mora, web development by Stas Bondar, launch film *The Orator* directed by Jono Seneff, motion design by Catherine Camiré.
- **Earlier identity (not this case):** Goodside Studio made Primer's previous brand, for the earlier company that ran online clubs plus in-person microschools and dated its founding to 2020. That brand spoke directly to children in open questions and short, snappy lines, and used a vibrant gradient palette with custom illustrations by Paulina Almira. newkid replaced it: the name is the only thing that carried over. (Primer's own materials now say "Est. 2019".)

## Brief & business truth

Primer runs small schools built around a teacher who knows each child: mastery before advancement, core academics in the morning, self-directed afternoon projects called "Pursuits", and technology used only where it helps learning (MAP testing, progress software). Tuition is low or covered by state scholarships, and schools in Texas are free. The audience is families first, but the site also has to work for investors, press and prospective Primer Leaders. With national expansion under way, the old brand (made for an online-clubs startup, soft and illustrated) could not carry the ambition of a "movement". newkid did the strategy, the identity, the website and the launch film.

## Strategy / insight

The category is full of ed-tech optimism and "innovation theatre". Primer's actual offer runs the other way: bring back what worked. The risk was that this reads as nostalgia. The platform **"School for What's Ahead"** turns the tension around and presents a return to fundamentals as the most serious preparation for the future ("Begin with what lasts. Build for what's ahead."). The founding belief is that if you take kids seriously, they take themselves seriously. newkid named the two visual worlds it refused: heraldic crests and library palettes on one side, app-like interface aesthetics on the other.

## The idea (one sentence) and its source

**Idea:** a primer is the first book, the building blocks of learning, so the whole identity is built from blocks: a graph-paper grid whose squares children fill in and that opens up as it grows, signed off by a square blue full stop that is both the end of a sentence and the first block.
**Source:** the name (primer as the first reader, and "building blocks" as the fundamentals), plus the school's craft (graph paper, exercise books, finishing a sentence properly).

## Register

Editorial schoolbook, heritage meets modern: a bookish flared serif, Swiss-style gridded layouts, a bright block palette and documentary portraits of real children. Serious, never cute.

## Category codes it used or broke

- **Broke the academic crest code:** no shield, no engraving and no library burgundy or navy, even though "the one-room schoolhouse" and "primers" invited all of them.
- **Broke the ed-tech code:** no gradients, no UI-style illustration and no rounded app look (which is exactly what Goodside's earlier Primer identity had).
- **Broke the children's-education code of cartoon primaries:** the four colours are tuned warmer and more sophisticated than straight primaries, and **red is excluded outright** because in school it means a marked-up test.
- **Broke the stock-photo code:** a public rule of no models, made into advertising copy.
- **Used, and made precise:** the school's own tools, namely graph paper, pencils, grade bands (K-2, 3-5, 6-8), the capitalised sentence with its full stop, and the primer book itself (the film and timeline show old primer covers).

## Mark

- **Type:** a wordmark with a coloured full stop: "Primer." (the period is part of the logo).
- **Construction:** "Primer" in upper and lower case in Sirca (Medium, per Fonts In Use), set tight. It is followed by a **square** full stop, not a round one, sitting on the baseline, about as wide as the letter stems, and in blue (#3B80E8 sampled) when the word is black on white or photography. The square period ties the word to the grid: it is a single grid cell at the smallest scale.
- **Construction drawing** (a published frame): hairline guides at the cap height and baseline, with small grey squares at the four corners of the bounding box. The squares look like the same size as the period, which suggests the period is the clearspace unit (inferred).
- **Optical details:** the dot of the i is round and sits high. The serifs flare and are wedge-cut, and the joins in m and r are sharp. The P's bowl is large, with a short, compact x-height relationship.
- **Variants:** black wordmark with a blue period on white; white wordmark with a blue period over photography, set huge and bleeding across the bottom of portraits; black wordmark with a black period on green or blue fields (the period turns the ink colour on coloured grounds); a white wordmark with a blue period on a pole banner, rotated 90°; a wordmark stacked over a campus address on the flag. A small-size "Primer" without the period appears inside some gridded social layouts (inferred to be an allowed simplification).
- **Animation:** the period is the starting cell that builds out into the grid (newkid says its simplicity opens it to storytelling and animation).

## Wordmark / lettering

The face is Sirca by Edito Type (Paris; designed by Alan Madić, with spacing and kerning by Igino Marini). It is a re-reading of Walter Käch's 1949 serif display models, and was still unreleased when newkid used it; Edito shows a 2026 release with Display and Text styles and italics. Sirca was chosen over serifs that felt too institutional or too decorative because it has a subtle flare, compact proportions, and a mix of geometric structure and humanist warmth. The wordmark is upper and lower case, tracked tight and in Medium weight. No letters were custom-modified beyond setting and the square period (inferred from the images).

## Type system

- **Sirca (serif):** the wordmark, and display headlines in decks, ads and posters. Headlines are Title Case and end in a full stop ("A First Look at the New Primer Brand.", "This Is What School Should Look Like."). The template deck literally tells the user to write capitalised sentences and end them with a dot. It also sets campaign typography such as the "100% Real Primer Students. 0% Models." ad, and "Est. 2019".
- **Söhne (Klim), Buch and heavier cuts:** body copy, interface, buttons and sentence-case sub-headlines ("We take kids and their education seriously."). Above all it sets the **giant grade numerals** "K-2", "3-5" and "6-8", which fill half a poster or a whole sign and act as wayfinding.
- **Hierarchy is restrained:** Fonts In Use notes that the site's wordmark (~32px) and section heads (~48px) avoid big contrast jumps. The drama comes from the scale of the numerals and the photography, not from headline size.
- **Type as image:** the wordmark is cropped off the bottom edge of portraits, and in the "Alphabet. Arithmetic. Agency." posts each word ends in a coloured period, so the period becomes a repeating accent (yellow in that case).

## Device(s) / graphic language

1. **The graph-paper grid.** A field of equal square cells drawn in thin white or black hairlines (on the orange poster, about 7–8 columns across). Individual cells are filled solid with another palette colour (one blue, one yellow and one white cell on orange), like a child colouring squares on squared paper. Coloured blocks can span 1×1, 1×n strips, or larger rectangles of several cells.
2. **The opening grid.** In the grade posters and digital layouts, the grid "opens up as it extends". Columns and rows become unequal: a wide column, a narrow one, a wide one, and rows of different heights. Some cells are crossed by single diagonal lines running corner to corner, so the regular squares turn into free, larger constructions. This stands for the agency a child gains once the fundamentals are in place. Thin hairlines of the grid often run straight through photographs and past their edges, so the image sits inside the construction.
3. **The block layout.** Every layout (web, deck, social, sign) is built as panels of the grid: photo cells, text cells, solid colour cells and empty cells, with 1px rules between them. On the web the grid lines stay visible as a structural decoration.
4. **The square period.** It is the smallest block and the brand's sign-off, used at the end of headlines and as a coloured accent. It is animated as the seed that builds the grid.
5. **Colour frames.** On cohort wall signs, coloured bars (for example green top, orange left, yellow right) frame a big grade numeral in Söhne on white, like blocks stacked around a label.

## Imagery

- **Rule:** no models, ever. Every image is a real Primer student at a real Primer school, shot by Luis Mora. The rule is itself a campaign ("100% Real Primer Students. 0% Models. This is what school should look like.").
- **Two modes:** (1) studio portraits on plain seamless backdrops (cream, pale grey or saturated blue), direct to camera, with confident poses and play props such as building bricks, markers and books, lit softly with a natural, slightly warm grade. (2) Documentary coverage in classrooms, on the blacktop, behind desks and in groups, including black-and-white candids in the film.
- **Cutouts:** full-length cut-out children placed between lines of headline type.
- **Crop:** tight portraits for cohort posts; full-body group line-ups for billboards; images placed inside grid cells.
- **Never:** stock imagery, adult actors, screens-first ed-tech shots or staged "diverse classroom" clichés (implied by the brief).
- **Film:** in *The Orator*, a student, MarQuis, delivers an adapted manifesto from a podium in his own cadence, with no founder and no voiceover.

## Voice

- Short, declarative sentences in the manner of primer textbooks and oratory. The studio aimed for the depth of an essay with the poetry of a classic speech.
- **Sample lines:** "School for What's Ahead." / "Begin with what lasts. Build for what's ahead." / "Alphabet. Arithmetic. Agency." / "This is what school should look like." / "We take kids and their education seriously." / "Every child deserves a strong beginning." / "The Building Blocks of a Timeless Education." / "The Gravity of Words."
- **Rules:** headlines end with a full stop (the period doubles as the logo). Use triads of fundamentals (alphabet, arithmetic, and agency or autonomy). Pair history with the future (the site's timeline runs from 1657 through 1779 and 1837 to today). Name things plainly: grades, Pursuits, Primer Leaders instead of teachers.
- **Limits:** no ed-tech buzzwords and no innovation theatre. Avoid cute talk to children (the reverse of the previous brand), and address parents, and children, as serious people.

## Applications

The launch film *The Orator*; a website (homepage, grade cohort pages, company timeline, approach, admissions); campus flag with the address; vertical pole banners; square cohort wall signs; posters (orange graph-paper, a white photo poster with a huge wordmark, blue and green grade grids); a roadside billboard with a line-up of students; digital screens with "School for What's Ahead"; social posts and stories; mobile layouts ("The Gravity of Words"); a presentation template system (dozens of slide layouts); folders colour-coded by programme (green Alphabet, yellow Arithmetic, orange Agency, Pursuits); and pencils in blue, yellow and orange printed with "Primer. School For What's Ahead." on a graph-paper wrap.
**What varies:** background colour (one field colour per piece), how open or strict the grid is, which cells are filled, photo versus type-led layouts, and the cohort numeral.
**What stays fixed:** the Sirca wordmark with its square period, hairline grid rules, the palette of four colours (no red), Söhne for information, real-student photography, and full-stop headlines.

## What makes it ownable

The square blue full stop after "Primer"; Sirca, a then-unreleased face, which nobody else had; graph-paper cells filled in with a warm four-colour palette that has no red; giant Söhne grade numerals as wayfinding; the grid that opens up with diagonal lines; and the public no-models promise, carried by real students.

## What a generic version would have been

Either a crest with a torch or open book, a Trajan-like serif, navy and burgundy, and "Excellence since…" copy; or an ed-tech startup look with a rounded sans, a gradient, a cheerful blob illustration, bright primaries including red, and stock photos of smiling kids on tablets, with a slogan about "the future of learning". The earlier Goodside identity sat close to the second of these.

## Principles it demonstrates

1. Build the whole grid from the literal meaning of the name, so each layout keeps retelling the idea.
2. Make the smallest possible element (a full stop) the logo's signature, and make it the seed of the graphic system too.
3. When the category splits into two clichés, name both and build the middle ground from the business's own tools (here graph paper, pencils and grade bands).
4. Cut the colour that carries a negative meaning in the category (red for marked tests), even if it would complete a primary set.
5. Turn an imagery rule into a public promise ("0% models") so the photography becomes proof, not decoration.
6. Let a grid show progress: start strict and regular, then open it up, so the system visibly grows the way the audience does.
