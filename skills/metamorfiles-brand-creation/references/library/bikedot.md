# Bikedot

- **Name / business / place / year / studio:** Bikedot, a high-end one-stop shop for cycling (curating and building bikes, parts and clothing, plus financing and insurance), online and from a London showroom. Identity 2024. Studio Sutherl& (Jim Sutherland, Ethan Brown) with the creative agency errror (Kim Papworth, Tony Davidson) on design and art direction. D&AD Graphite Pencil 2024 (Branding; Magazine & Newspaper Design; Graphic Design).

## Brief & business truth
Founders with about 30 years' experience with leading bike builders, selling curated premium bikes and kit, including to companies (cycle-to-work). Audience: enthusiasts and commuters who want the best British makers without having to be a "bike geek" themselves (from the paper's masthead copy). The shop sells many brands, so the identity had to be a stage that sits under other people's logos (Brooks, Middleburn, Evari appear in applications) without competing with them.

## Strategy / insight
Build the brand out of the one thing every bike on the floor has in common: a tyre and the mark it leaves. The identity is literally printed by rolling, so the brand is made by the act of cycling. Restraint (black and white, two typefaces) lets the physical print carry the character.

## The idea (one sentence) and its source
The word BIKE is cut into tyre-tread rollers, so every Bikedot surface carries the track a bike would leave. **Source:** product-craft (the tyre and its track), with the name supplying the letters.

## Register
Raw vernacular print held inside a strict monochrome grid: industrial, hand-made, newsprint.

## Category codes it used or broke
- Broke: bike-shop codes of hero-shot speed photography, neon or team colours, lycra lifestyle, carbon-texture "tech" graphics and swooshes.
- Used: the tyre track (a universal cycling sign) and race-number numerals (the giant "1" on issue 01), recast as typography rather than illustration.

## Mark
- Wordmark only: lowercase "bikedot" set tight in a geometric sans (Euclid Circular A per the colophon), bold weight. The "o" of "dot" is filled into a solid black disc, the one modification; the dot is at x-height, the same diameter as the other bowls.
- The solid disc is also the standalone symbol ("the dot": a standard of excellence, per BP&O). It scales to a giant circle filling most of a newspaper cover, and to a small bullet next to partner names (a disc before the Middleburn wordmark).
- Variants: wordmark alone; disc alone; wordmark with tracks running through it (hoarding).

## Wordmark / lettering
- Wordmark: lowercase, bold geometric sans, tight tracking, no ligatures beyond the filled "o".
- Track alphabet: eight "Tracks" (Track 01–08 on the specimen sheet), each a different modular construction of the letters B-I-K-E repeated end to end in a continuous band, like a tread. Modules: Track 01 chevrons and arrowheads; Tracks 02–03 letters mirrored top and bottom (the band reads from both edges, like a symmetrical tread); Tracks 04–06 stadium shapes and half-rounds (pill modules, straight stems, filled bowls); Track 07 quarter-circle and rectangle cuts; Track 08 a fine grid of small rounded blocks. All are heavy black stencil-like shapes with narrow white cuts, tuned to read as tread first and letters second.

## Type system
- Euclid Circular A: wordmark, mastheads, captions, colophons, all small text; sentence case, regular to bold.
- MFred 2.0 (Matt Willey): condensed display for newspaper headlines, set huge and tight, uppercase ("SHRED.", "CYCLE TO WORK / WORK TO CYCLE", "EVARI"), sometimes cropped by a photograph cutting through the word.
- Track alphabet: pattern and texture, never for reading copy.
- Type-as-image moves: a giant race-number numeral as the cover; a headline split by a cut-out photo; words overprinted by track marks.

## Colour system
Black and white only (newsprint off-white in print). The hand-rolled prints add greys where ink thins at the edges. Reflective silver appears only as a material on apparel. No accent colour (inferred as a rule from every application).

## Device(s) / graphic language
1. **Tyre-track rollers:** custom rollers with interchangeable rings, each ring carrying one Track, run by hand across paper, walls and floors. Tracks cross at any angle, curve in arcs (the path of a wheel), fade as ink runs out, overlap each other and the logo. Every printed copy is unique.
2. **Digital tracks:** the same eight Tracks set as clean straight bands for tape, labels, straps and swing tags.
3. **The dot:** a solid black circle, used at extreme scale (poster, cover) or as a tiny bullet; photographs are sometimes set inside or beside it like a wheel or a lens.
4. **Tracks as wayfinding:** printed on the showroom floor where bikes park, so real tyres sit on printed tyre marks.

## Imagery
Black-and-white photography only: products shot as objects on pale grey sweeps (saddle, chainset, shoes), and grainy motion shots of riders at night lit by reflective strips. Products are often "ridden" by a track running out from them. Never colour, never lifestyle stock, never speed-blur hero shots in team kit (inferred from the set).

## Voice
Plain, direct, a little wry: "Cycle to work / Work to cycle", "SHRED." Explains the offer without jargon (the masthead says you no longer need to be a bike geek). Short headlines, editorial long copy inside the paper.

## Applications
Newsprint magazine (cover, spreads, colophon), posters, showroom hoarding and floor, swing tags and tape, tote bag with track-printed straps, reflective track tape on jackets and bike frames, catalogues, partner product shots (Brooks saddle box overprinted), website domain bike.co.uk.
- **Fixed:** black and white, the wordmark, the two typefaces, the dot.
- **Varies:** which Track, its angle, curve and density; hand-rolled (textured, unique) versus digital (clean); scale of the dot; the headline face's crops.

## Guidelines structure
No published guidelines. The track specimen sheet (Track 01–08 plus wordmark) acts as the core system page.

## What makes it ownable
The tread made of the word BIKE; the physical rolling process and its imperfect ink; the filled "o" dot. A strip of track alone identifies the brand, even on a strap or a jacket seam.

## What a generic version would have been
A bold italic sans wordmark with a speed swoosh or a chainring icon, a signal colour (orange or electric green), hero photography of riders on mountain roads, and product grids. Indistinguishable from any bike retailer or brand it stocks.

## Principles it demonstrates
- Make the brand's pattern with the product's own action, so the craft story is literally true.
- Encode the name in the pattern so texture doubles as signature.
- For a multi-brand retailer, stay monochrome so stocked brands' colours never clash and the store's system still reads.
- Let one tiny modification (a filled letter) carry the whole mark, and reuse that shape as the symbol.
- Keep analogue imperfection (fading ink, unique copies) inside a strict digital grid to get warmth without chaos.
- Ship a family of variants (eight tracks) so the device never repeats exactly.
