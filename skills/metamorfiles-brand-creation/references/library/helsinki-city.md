# City of Helsinki — visual identity

## Name / business / place / year / studio

- **Name:** City of Helsinki (Helsingin kaupunki / Helsingfors stad), the "Helsinki framed logo" system
- **Business:** municipal government: every service, department, venue, project and event the city runs (about 40,000 employees), plus city marketing and tourism
- **Place:** Helsinki, Finland (bilingual city: Finnish and Swedish)
- **Year:** strategy and design 2017 (7 months, city decision HEL 2017-002450); public rollout from early 2018 (inferred from press dates: Vimeo launch film, BP&O April 2018); guidelines still maintained, with Helsinki Grotesk Pro added in 2025
- **Studio:** Werklig (Helsinki). Typeface Helsinki Grotesk by Camelot Typefaces (Wolfgang Schwärzler), a custom cut of Gräbenbach. Photography Jussi Hellsten, producer Hanna Kaikula; Oodi signage photography Martin Sommerschield / Kuvio. Digital system later carried on by the city's Helsinki Design System (HDS).

## Brief & business truth

Helsinki's 2017 city strategy set the goal of being the most functional city in the world. Visually the city was the opposite: every department, venue, school, project and event had its own logo (BP&O shows about fifty of them, from zoo to employment services), so residents could not tell which services the city itself provided and the city got no credit for them. The only shared symbol was the 400-year-old coat of arms (a crowned golden wooden boat on white waves in a blue shield), too detailed and ceremonial for daily use.

The identity had to work for 40,000 employees who are not designers, on everything from building-site hoardings and custody notices to international tourism, and speak to residents, all Finns, immigrants, tourists and dignitaries, in Finnish, Swedish, English and many other scripts. Werklig won the pitch with a co-creation process: about 200 city staff took part in sprints and workshops over seven months. Reported results: a 19% rise in visitors the following year, thousands of person-hours saved yearly through one system, and higher satisfaction with services now that people can see they are the city's.

## Strategy / insight

- Strategy pillars investigated with staff: Fascinating Contrasts, Unique & Diverse, Transformation & Growth, Helsinki Works. Brand concept line in the guidelines: "One Hel of an Impact" (a pun on the local nickname Hel).
- Insight: the city already owned one symbol everyone recognised, the crest. Keep its silhouette and its waves, drop the heraldic detail, and the new system feels "excitingly new and reassuringly old at the same time" (Werklig). Familiarity makes employees and residents adopt it rather than resist it.
- Functional insight: a public organisation this size needs one container that every unit and every language can go into, not a family of logos.

## The idea (one sentence) and its source

**Idea:** the outline of Helsinki's crest becomes an adaptive frame that holds any name in any language, and the crest's waves become a surface-dividing edge that cuts every layout into contrasting halves.

**Source:** place (the city's own coat of arms, plus colours taken from the city's streets, trams, cathedral and fortress).

## Register

Civic modular/functional: a strict, rule-driven kit (one frame, one typeface, six wave edges, 14 flat colours, a grid) that non-designers can run, made warm by loud flat colour, candid photography and the soft wave edge.

## Category codes it used or broke, and how

- **Used:** the heraldic shield as civic authority (kept as silhouette only), the city name as the logo, blue from the crest, bilingual Finnish/Swedish versions, pictogram wayfinding.
- **Broke:** city-branding clichés of a skyline, cathedral dome or landmark silhouette with a tourism slogan; the "destination" gradient-and-sunset look; one logo per department. It also broke municipal greyness: saturated, sometimes clashing colour pairs (pink on red, blue on green) are allowed on large surfaces, and photography shows construction sites, bad weather and ordinary suburbs rather than postcard views.

## Mark

**Type:** framed wordmark (a container/emblem hybrid; the frame is the recognisable part, the text inside changes).

**Construction** (measured from the city's own SVG, 130 x 60 units, medium size):
- A monoline outline drawn with one constant stroke of about 3.8 units (about 2.9% of the logo's width). Guidelines tie the stroke weight to the stem of the letter H in the wordmark (the stroke matches the letter weight, inferred as roughly equal).
- Flat top edge, straight vertical sides, bottom corners rounded with an outer radius of about 16 units (about 12 inside), i.e. roughly a quarter of the logo's height.
- At the centre of the bottom edge the line splits into two concave arcs that meet in a single downward point, 6 units below the bottom line (about 10% of total height), spanning about 18% of the width. This is the tip of the crest's shield base, and it makes the frame read as a speech bubble with a tail (inferred reading; the guidelines call it only "the framed logo" and define no separate speech-bubble device).
- The wordmark "Helsinki" sits inside, optically centred in the rectangular body (text centre about y 26.6 of a 54-unit body), spanning about 72% of the width. The height of the capital H sets the inner margin from text to frame on the left and top (measured about 17–18 units, equal to the cap height).
- The proportion of text to frame is locked and the placement of text in the frame is standardised.

**Variants:** Helsinki (primary, used for Finnish, English and international), Helsingfors (Swedish-only materials), bilingual Helsinki/Helsingfors stacked on two lines in a taller frame (only fully bilingual materials), Helsinki Finland (international marketing), Pride versions, and other-language versions (Russian, Arabic, Chinese, Japanese, Korean shown) made only by the official animation rule: the frame stretches horizontally and vertically around the text while stroke, corner radius and tail stay fixed. The original 2017 system also put unit names inside the frame (Oodi, Kisahalli, Arbis, Stoa, Päiväkoti Jätkäsaari); the current guidelines instead set a unit's name in Helsinki Grotesk beside the framed Helsinki logo and forbid separate unit logos (an evolution inferred from comparing Werklig's 2017 images with today's rules). Endorsement lockups: "With love from Helsinki", "Together with Helsinki", "Supported by Helsinki", horizontal or vertical, in three languages.

**Sizes and space:** minimum 25 mm wide in print, 200 px on screen, no maximum. Protected area = H height around the logo (2 x H height for the large version, 1 x H as absolute minimum). Six standard positions: four corners and centre (top and bottom).

**Colour:** primarily black or white; brand-colour versions allowed on white (or black). Frame and text are always one colour; never a solid-filled frame.

**Forbidden:** stretching, rotating, effects (shadow, glow, 3D), placing it on or inside another element, two colours in one logo, low-contrast backgrounds, a bilingual logo in a one-language material, more than one logo version per view, and the coat of arms together with the framed logo (the crest is kept for ceremonial use only: state visits, seals, medals, diplomas).

## Wordmark / lettering

Set in Helsinki Grotesk, bold weight (inferred from comparison with the Bold style), capital H plus lower case, tight tracking, no modified letters apart from the typeface's own details. The same face sets every language version, so the lettering is never drawn separately. Unit names set beside the logo follow the same recipe: sentence case, tracking −25, leading 100% of size, Bold (Medium allowed, Black for names under 10 letters), broken over 2–3 lines at most with no hyphenation, and never smaller than the word Helsinki in the logo.

## Type system

- **Helsinki Grotesk** (Camelot Typefaces, Wolfgang Schwärzler): a custom version of Gräbenbach, a grotesque that draws on early grotesques and brush-painted signs. The city's version carries small wavy details in its terminals and serifs that echo the frame's bows and the wave motifs (which letters show them most is not documented in what I could read). Originally Regular, Medium, Bold, Black, each with italic; since 2025 Helsinki Grotesk Pro adds Light and a variable version. Licensed per style (€65–70) from Camelot.
- **Arial** in Office applications (the OTF font doesn't work there) and for Arabic; **Noto Sans** for Chinese, Japanese and other scripts the font lacks.
- **Rules:** headings have leading equal to size (30/30, subhead 15/15); body text 120% (10/12). Upper and lower case always; no all-caps titles or text. Text is never decorated or made into illustration. Left alignment preferred. Signage uses Medium only; video uses Regular and Bold (with italics) only.
- **Type as image:** big type alone on a flat colour or a full-bleed photo is an approved cover and poster approach (type-only covers, large pull quotes on colour), e.g. the 2017 annual report cover "Vuosi-kertomus 2017" set huge in white on Coat of Arms blue.

- **Roles:** black and white carry the logo, all signage text and most type. The 12 hues are flat fields for surfaces, wave divisions, covers and cards. Gold and Silver may be foil or metallic prints. Copper (a bright mint, named after oxidised copper roofs) and Engel (the pale yellow of Engel's Empire buildings) are the most distinctive.
- **Hard rules:** 100% values only; no tints, pastels, mixes, gradients, translucency or effects (infographics excepted). Grey and black are not used as percentages.
- **Logo on colour:** white logo on any colour except Engel and Silver; black logo on any colour except Brick, Bus and Coat of Arms.
- **Pairs:** a published colour matrix lists allowed combinations. "Demanding" high-vibration pairs (such as pink with red or orange, blue with green) are allowed only as grouped colour surfaces and large backgrounds, never for information or small text; the guidelines treat these clashing pairs as part of what makes Helsinki's communication its own. - **Share (inferred from applications):** mostly one or two saturated fields per surface plus black or white type; no application shows more than two or three hues together except the palette overview and card sets.

## Device(s) / graphic language

**1. Wave motifs (koro, plural korot):** the white waves under the boat in the crest, turned into six edge profiles. Each is a repeating edge between two surfaces, not a line or ornament.

Geometry (from the HDS source; one period is 32 units wide in "Dense" and 96 px in "Default", i.e. ×3, in a band 85 px tall):
- **Tyyni / Calm:** straight edge (amplitude 0); the only one allowed as a plain divider.
- **Perus / Basic:** smooth scallop, two symmetric S-curves rising to a rounded crest; amplitude 5 per 32 (about 1:6.4).
- **Pulssi / Pulse:** the same scallop, twice as deep; amplitude 10 per 32.
- **Tyrsky / Wave:** a pointed crest: each side curves in and tightens into a sharp tip at the top, with wide rounded troughs between tips; amplitude 10 per 32.
- **Syke / Beat:** tall, round-topped teeth with straight flanks; amplitude about 21 per 32 (the deepest).
- **Värinä / Vibration:** a zigzag of straight 45° sides, a right-angled V; amplitude 16 per 32.

Rules:
- One motif per view; don't overuse it, and plain colour or full-bleed photo with type is an equally valid layout.
- Divide surfaces only horizontally, vertically or at 45° (HDS allows every 45° step, flipped as needed), placed on a grid that works across A4 portrait and landscape and other formats.
- At least two full wave tips visible per surface; always cut the motif at a crest or at a trough, never mid-slope.
- Use only the original vector files; they may be split, cropped or duplicated but never redrawn, and no look-alike waves may be created; never used as a line, hyphen or pattern.
- A koro can mask an image (photo inside the wave shape), separate text from image, or divide two images or an image and a colour field. In HDS it edges heroes and footers, one size and one shape per service, and is never used as an accent or active-state indicator.
- Animated versions exist (the edge scrolls horizontally).
- In the 2017 applications the wave is also scaled up very large so that a few huge, soft crests cross a card or cover diagonally (the business cards and the flag show this).

**2. The frame as container / speech bubble:** the framed logo doubles as a labelling device: a recognisable bubble shape into which the city speaks a name in any script, and which stays identifiable even when a reader cannot read the text inside (Werklig's stated aim). Illustrative icons are also built from the frame's outline and the wave language.

**3. Surface division grid:** a grid that sets where a straight, wave or diagonal cut may fall, and where photos, colour fields and type go. Image surfaces pair on purpose: image with colour field, two images contrasting in scale or colour, two images of contrasting weather, or a historical photo beside a contemporary one.

**4. Pictograms:** 311 signage pictograms, filled shapes, no rounded line ends, with the wave detail as their character, labelled in Helsinki Grotesk Medium; plus UI icons in HDS and a separate illustrative icon set (never used as signs).

## Imagery

- **Photography:** candid, unstaged, without filters: Helsinki shown the way it really is. Subjects are people and feelings: residents, staff, businesses and visitors in everyday life, working together, small funny details; neighbourhoods beyond the centre; people of different ages, backgrounds, bodies and abilities; urban nature; the city under construction. Themes: Changing Helsinki, Functional Helsinki, Helsinki of contrasts (summer/winter, nature/city, calm/beat, science/art, rough edges), Original Helsinki, plus historical archive photos.
- **Light and camera:** seasons and weather visible, including evening, storm and snow; motion blur welcome; surprising angles; shallow depth of field and reflections for sensitive subjects to keep people unrecognisable. Edits must look natural and timeless; black-and-white only for historical images.
- **Never:** stereotypes, staged posing, prettified generic stock, only-happy-smiling people, vague symbolic images for sensitive topics, earth-in-hands clichés.
- **Illustration:** relaxed and not too realistic, always in the palette, clear strong styles; no classical, rustic-romantic or fairy-tale looks, no over-abstraction; diverse people shown as active subjects, with recognisable Helsinki details.
- AI-generated or heavily modified content must carry a watermark and a disclosure line.

## Voice

The guidelines publish little on verbal tone; what exists:
- Brand concept "One Hel of an Impact" (playful pun on the city's nickname), later a brand line about the good life in Helsinki.
- Endorsement lines are fixed phrases, not invented per case: "With love from Helsinki" (foundation-run culture and leisure brands), "Together with Helsinki" (city-group units, partners), "Supported by Helsinki" (grant recipients).
- Language rules are the voice: Finnish and Swedish treated as equals (Helsingfors versions mandatory in bilingual communication to residents), separate lines per language on signs, plain sentence case, no shouting capitals.
- Headlines in applications are short, factual and service-led (e.g. an annual report titled simply "Annual report 2017"; a metro screen advertising 2,594 adult-education courses in plain words) (inferred from the applications).

## Applications

Shown: business cards and stationery; city flags; tote bags and water bottles; annual report and brochure covers; cultural-centre programme covers (Vuotalo, Annantalo); digital posters on metro platforms; wayfinding and signage at Oodi central library (black/white panels on wood, floor numbers, maps); illuminated info sign; pictograms on doors; unit and language versions of the frame; building facades; video and animation; the HDS web components (koro heroes and footers).

**What stays fixed:** the frame and its proportions; Helsinki Grotesk; 100% flat palette colours; black or white logo; one koro per view cut at crest or trough; left-aligned, spacious layouts.

**What varies:** the koro shape (six choices) and its orientation (horizontal, vertical, 45°) and scale; the colour pair (from quiet Fog/Coat of Arms to loud pink/red); photo versus colour field; the text inside or next to the frame (unit name, language, script); the frame's width and height. Signage strips it down to black, white and Medium weight; marketing and covers turn up colour and the wave; digital services take it through HDS tokens.

## What makes it ownable

- The frame alone: flat top, rounded lower corners and the small pointed tail at the bottom centre. It is recognisable empty, full of Cyrillic or Arabic, or at 200 px.
- The six named wave edges cutting surfaces at 0/45/90°, especially the scallop against a flat colour.
- The city-sourced palette with its local names (Tram, Metro, Bus, Engel, Suomenlinna, Copper) and its licensed clashes such as pink against red.
- Helsinki Grotesk's slightly wavy grotesque letters, the same in every language version.
- Candid photography of ordinary, weathered Helsinki in place of postcards.

## What a generic version would have been

A new "Helsinki" wordmark in a neutral geometric sans with a skyline or Cathedral-dome silhouette, a Baltic-blue wave swoosh, a tourism tagline, gradient sunsets and staged stock photos of happy people on the waterfront, while each department kept its own logo and the crest stayed untouched as a separate, unused heirloom. That version would have looked like any Nordic destination and solved nothing for the 40,000 staff.

## Principles it demonstrates

1. Mine the one symbol the public already recognises, keep only its silhouette, and make that the system, so that the new identity feels familiar from the first day.
2. Make the mark a container whose fixed parts (stroke, corners, tail) carry recognition, so that any name, language or script can go inside without making a new logo.
3. Turn a secondary detail of the source symbol into a small set of named, geometrically defined edge profiles, and give them strict cutting rules (angles, whole tips, crest or trough) instead of letting them become ornament.
4. Take colours from the place's real objects, name them after those objects, allow only flat 100% values, and publish exactly which pairs are safe for information and which clashes are allowed only on large surfaces.
5. Write rules a non-designer can follow (one logo and one motif per view, black or white for signs, fixed endorsement phrases), because a system for 40,000 people is only as good as its most careless user.
6. Make the photography honest about the place (weather, suburbs, construction), so that it contrasts with the tidy geometry instead of copying it.
