# National Portrait Gallery

- **Name:** National Portrait Gallery (NPG)
- **Business:** national museum of portraits of people who shaped Britain, from Tudor paintings to contemporary photography; free admission; shop, café, restaurant, learning centre, events, membership
- **Place:** London, UK
- **Year:** 2023 (revealed January 2023; launched with the reopening on 22 June 2023 after a three-year, £41m "Inspiring People" rebuild)
- **Studio:** Edit Brand Studio (Manchester; Karen Hughes, Adrian Newell) with strategy by Boardroom Consulting; monogram and logotype hand-drawn by lettering artist Peter Horridge; typeface NPG Serif by Monotype (Tom Foley, Jordan Bell)

## Brief & business truth
The gallery's identity had not been updated for about 20 years and it under-performed against London's other big museums. The rebuild and reopening were the moment to relaunch it as a gallery "of people, for people" (quoted from the gallery's statement) telling Britain's story through faces. Constraints: the work (portraits) must always lead; the brand has to sit over and beside paintings and photographs of every era without fighting them; it has to stretch from Tube posters to café aprons and a fine-dining restaurant.

## Strategy / insight
The gallery's own history already contained its mark: an NPG monogram appears in the building's railings, furniture and mosaics, and the first director George Scharf sketched the initials entwined in a circle in an 1893 workbook. Reviving the house's own hand makes the new identity feel inherited, not imposed, while a bright modern colour palette and big verbs make it about visitors doing things with people's stories.

## The idea (one sentence)
The first director's 1893 doodle of the entwined initials, redrawn as a confident monogram, becomes the gallery's signature, framing portraits of people in colours drawn from the building and its paint archive.
**Source:** people (a person's own hand from the archive, and a collection that is literally people), with the place (building details) as supporting source.

## Register
Heritage-contemporary editorial: a calligraphic serif monogram, a custom serif, full-bleed portrait imagery, electric accent colours.

## Category codes used or broken
- Used: serif type and a monogram (heritage institution), artwork as hero.
- Broken: instead of quiet museum neutrals (black, white, grey) it uses fluorescent mint, electric yellow and vermilion; instead of institutional titles, single imperative verbs set vertically (Join, Look, Learn); the monogram is cropped off the edges of posters and printed huge on umbrellas like a fashion label.

## Mark
- **Monogram:** N, P and G entwined inside a ring. The N is a serif capital with a thin diagonal; the P is raised above the N and shares/overlaps the N's right stem, its bowl sitting top-right; the G wraps beneath, its bowl crossing the N's diagonal and ending in a flat vertical spur at bottom right. Serifs are small, sharp and steeply bracketed (BP&O: subtle but steep), strokes contrasty like a broad pen. The ring is a softened oval, slightly taller than wide, with calligraphic modulation (thicker on the sides, thinner at top and bottom). Letters sit off-centre within the ring, weighted to the upper right.
- **Logotype:** NATIONAL PORTRAIT GALLERY in hand-drawn serif capitals, designed to be set on one, two or three lines so it can avoid covering an artwork. In motion it sits at the top of a thin-lined rounded rectangle that changes proportion like a picture frame (portrait to landscape).
- **Variants:** monogram alone (ring on or off), monogram cropped at the page edge, large-scale monogram as pattern (umbrella), embroidered monogram (apron), stacked logotype 1/2/3 lines.

## Wordmark / lettering
Hand-drawn by Peter Horridge from Scharf's spidery pen sketch, thickened into robust forms. Capitals only in the logotype; letter shapes echo NPG Serif.

## Type system
- **NPG Serif** (Monotype): three styles, Display plus Text Regular and Text Medium. Classical proportions with an Art Nouveau accent taken from a stone mosaic uncovered during the building works and from past gallery posters and identities. Tested large for signage and small for captions.
- **Inter** (secondary sans): occasional subheads and captions.
- Rules: alternate sentence case and all caps; headings may run vertically (rotated 90°, reading bottom-to-top along the right edge); stacked single-word lists centred or flush left (Book / Watch / Look / Listen / Meet / Drink / Shop / Dine; Play / Create / Make / Learn / Join / Book); colour used sparingly on type for emphasis.
- Type-as-image: lists of sitters' surnames (Bowie, Attenborough, Boleyn, Winehouse, Malala, Hockney, Turing, Pankhurst, Freud) stacked as a typographic poster.

## Colour system
Published description: a restrained hero palette of mint green, electric yellow, hot vermilion and cerulean blue, modernised from paints and materials in the building and archive. Sampled from studio images (approximate, inferred):
- Mint #37FEC3
- Electric yellow #F4F23D

- Cerulean blue (not sampled)
- Black, and white/stone neutrals
Roles: one accent per piece; accents used for the monogram, headline type or a flat panel beside an artwork; chosen so type and monogram contrast over any portrait without dominating it. Solid accent fields for digital and merch.

## Device(s) / graphic language
- **Cropped monogram:** ring bled off the top-right corner of posters as a corner stamp.
- **Vertical verb:** one large imperative word in NPG Serif Display, rotated, in an accent colour, running up the right side of a full-bleed portrait.
- **List stacks:** short lines of offers or names, one per line (membership "Me & …" lists: Me & My heroes, Me & The experts).
- **Portrait frame:** thin rounded-corner rectangle that holds the logotype and changes aspect ratio in motion (inferred to reference picture frames).

## Imagery
The collection itself: historic oil portraits and contemporary photographic portraits, always full-bleed or large, never cropped through the face; sitters of every era mixed side by side (Byron, Maisie Williams, Mary Seacole on one set). No stock photography, no photos of visitors as hero.

## Voice
Warm, active, first and second person; single verbs as calls to action (Join, Look, Learn, Book, Meet); membership framed as "Me &" relationships; admission line "Free admission — npg.org.uk". Emphasis on people's stories over art-historical language.

## Applications
Monogram, logotype, typeface, London Underground posters, What's On programme posters, membership card and materials, social films, website, signage (inferred from typeface testing), café aprons (embroidered monogram), t-shirts with portraits, umbrella with giant monogram, swing tags, shop products.
**What varies:** accent colour, portrait, verb, logotype line-breaks, frame proportion. **What stays fixed:** monogram drawing, NPG Serif, one-accent rule, portrait-first layouts.

## Guidelines structure
Not published. Components shown: monogram, logotype (1/2/3-line), NPG Serif family, palette, typographic behaviours (case alternation, vertical headings), motion frame, applications.

## What makes it ownable
The entwined NPG in its calligraphic ring (a genuine historic artefact of the institution), and the combination of old-master portraits with fluorescent mint or vermilion type.

## What a generic version would have been
A sans-serif wordmark "National Portrait Gallery" in black, a picture-frame outline icon, grey and white layouts, and a tagline like "Discover the faces of Britain".

## Principles it demonstrates
- Search the client's archive and building before drawing anything; a found artefact gives instant legitimacy.
- Let a historic form carry heritage and let colour carry modernity; one old, one new.
- When the content is the hero, design the brand to frame and contrast with it (one accent per piece, logotype that can re-break its lines).
- Give the voice verbs, not nouns: invitations to act make an institution feel "for people".
- Stretch the mark to lifestyle scale (umbrella, apron, crop) so it behaves like a fashion monogram, not a government seal.
