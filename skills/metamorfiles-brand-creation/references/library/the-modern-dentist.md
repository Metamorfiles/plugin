# The Modern Dentist

- **Name / business / place / year / studio:** The Modern Dentist, an independent private dental practice (general, cosmetic, orthodontics, implants) founded by Dr Mark Flynn. 1 Leonard Street, Shoreditch, London EC2, beside Old Street station. Practice opened December 2023 (its own About page; the website by Together Digital launched 18 Dec 2023); YourStudio published the identity on 13 May 2024. Studio: YourStudio (London; experience design and strategy), senior designer Tom Flint. Scope: brand strategy, visual and verbal identity, and the design of the practice interior.

## Brief & business truth

A new practice in a creative, young, professional district, competing with high-street NHS practices and glossy cosmetic clinics. Its clinical stance is minimal intervention: preserve natural teeth with non-invasive methods and modern materials rather than drill-and-fill. The category experience it had to beat is dread: impersonal service, lifeless waiting rooms, old methods. The brief was a strategy, an identity and a tone of voice that made that stance visible, plus a physical home where the brand is lived, not just printed. Audience (inferred): anxious or lapsed adult patients in East London who choose a dentist the way they choose a gym or a café.

## Strategy / insight

Promise: "Dentistry done differently." Two insights drive it. First, the clinical one: every great smile is unique, and so are the teeth that make it; the practice enhances natural beauty rather than standardising it. Second, the category audit: YourStudio found dental branding to be endless variations of white and blue, all equally forgettable. So the identity takes its forms from real teeth and its warmth from colours no dentist uses, and it speaks like a friend, not a clinic.

## The idea (one sentence) and its source

Teeth, drawn as soft geometric shapes, become the windows through which everything is seen (photos, words, doors, shelves), set in warm confectionery colours instead of clinical white and blue. Source: product-craft (the anatomy of teeth) combined with reversal of category codes (clinical colour and tone).

## Register

Soft, warm, playful-confident. Friendly consumer-lifestyle rather than medical: rounded geometry, tonal pastel grounds with deep same-hue type, big sentence-case headlines, cheerful puns. Restrained enough in layout (lots of flat ground, one shape, one line of type) to still read as competent.

## Category codes it used or broke

- Broke: white and blue, chrome, sterile photography of instruments and chairs, tooth-with-sparkle icons, smile swooshes, "excellence in dental care" copy, serious corporate serif or navy sans.
- Broke: the waiting room. The interior is coral, red, teal and timber, with lounge chairs and plants, not rows of vinyl seats.
- Used, but reframed: the tooth itself. Instead of a literal clip-art tooth, the identity uses tooth silhouettes abstracted into construction-grid shapes, so they read first as friendly blobs and arches and only second as teeth.
- Used: the smile. Photography is of laughing, natural faces, and every headline is a pun on smiles or teeth, but the smile is shown as personality, not as a before/after product shot.

## Mark

- **Type:** a container symbol plus a stacked wordmark (emblem-like lockup). The container is the arch, the incisor-derived member of the shape family.
- **Construction:** a symmetric arch drawn as a single outline: a semicircular top whose diameter equals the full width, straight parallel sides, and a flat base with two small radius corners (about a third of the top radius, inferred from the construction film). Proportion roughly 4 wide by 5 tall. The outline stroke matches the stroke weight of the mono type inside it, so container and letters read as one drawing.
- **Wordmark placement:** THE / MODERN / DENTIST in three lines, flush left, sitting in the lower-left of the arch, leaving the dome empty. The empty dome is the "tooth"; the text is its root. Line lengths step out (3, 6, 7 characters), producing a small stepped staircase inside the curve.
- **Variants seen:** outline arch in plum on light grounds; black outline on the lilac illuminated projecting sign; pink filled arch as a door sticker and opening-times holder; filled lilac arch on a plum disc as the Instagram avatar (icon variant with no words); wordmark-only is not shown (inferred: the arch is never dropped).
- **Small size:** the mono wordmark stays legible at footer size (billboard, bus shelter corner), because mono widths keep counters open.

## Wordmark / lettering

GT Pressura Mono (Grilli Type), uppercase, regular weight, default tracking, tight leading so the three lines form a block. Unmodified letters; the logic is the monospace grid, which gives even line texture that fits the arch's straight sides. It links to the display face because both are cuts of GT Pressura, whose corners are slightly rounded like the shapes.

## Type system

- **GT Pressura Extended:** the one voice for everything said: headlines, body, wayfinding, app screens, tote bags. Sentence case, regular to medium weight (inferred, never heavy black), tight leading around 1.0 to 1.1 at display sizes, flush left, set big on flat colour with generous empty ground. Its wide, geometric, soft-cornered forms echo the shapes.
- **GT Pressura Mono:** reserved for the wordmark.
- **Small UI labels:** uppercase at small size on buttons (BOOK A FREE CONSULTATION) (inferred as the face in caps).
- **Type-as-image moves:** type is set in the darkest shade of its ground's own hue (plum on lilac and pink, deep green on mint), so each panel is monochrome-tonal; headlines are the image on posters and windows; lettering is vinyl-cut directly onto glass and walls ("The home of happy teeth" in lilac on the fanlight).

No white-and-blue anywhere. Coral and red are mostly physical (desk, shelves, chairs, window); lilac and mint dominate print and screen.

## Device(s) / graphic language

- **Tooth shape system:** four shapes built on an 8 by 10 dotted grid from tangent circles joined by straight tangents (shown in the construction film): (1) a block with straight left side and base, small corners, and one great sweeping corner drawn from an oversized circle; (2) an asymmetric wedge (canine or premolar) from a six-unit top circle, two unequal lower circles and a small tip circle, tapering to a soft point; (3) the symmetric arch (incisor), top circle equal to the width, small base corners; (4) a molar: a six-unit top circle and two four-unit lower circles tangent at the centre, sides tapering outward, a shallow notch between the two lobes. They morph into each other in motion.
- **Shapes as windows:** a shape in a lighter tone of the ground holds a cut-out portrait (billboard, website hero), or is cropped to become a window for the photo itself (bus shelter arch). Shapes are usually oversized and cropped by the format edge, so only a corner or lobe is seen.
- **Line trace:** a thin plum or dark outline traces part of a shape's contour, offset from the filled shape (poster "Floss like a boss", coral shopfront window, social posts), adding a drawn, lighter layer.
- **Shapes in 3D:** the door panel is a plum arch; retail shelf signs are half-disc bent metal; the retail niche is an arch cut into a wall; the reception desk has a curved plan; the "Treatment Coordinator" door sign is a pink tooth blob.
- **Arrow:** a simple arrow in the type colour for wayfinding and calls to action.

## Imagery

Real people, real smiles: head-and-shoulders portraits of laughing or broadly smiling patients and the actual dentists, shot on plain light backgrounds and cut out so they sit inside a shape. Natural, even light, warm skin tones, casual clothing in saturated colours (mustard jumper, cream knit). Faces are frontal, eye contact, mouth open in a real laugh. What never appears (inferred from the whole set): instruments, dental chairs, gloved hands in mouths, x-rays, stock blue-white teeth close-ups.

## Voice

Positive, friendly, confident; short lines, puns on teeth and smiles, the vocabulary of pleasure rather than treatment. Sample lines from the applications: Dentistry done differently, The home of happy teeth, This way for pearly whites, Book now and give yourself something to smile about, Something to smile about (Opening soon), Floss like a boss, Less drilling, more thrilling, I love my dentist, You're all booked in!, Say hello to our friendly and talented team, We love your teeth. Swaps: "procedure" becomes a benefit, "patient" becomes you, "appointment confirmed" becomes a celebration. Limit (inferred): the jokes stay on posters, windows and merch; clinical information (the website body, prices) is plain and reassuring, still in the first person plural.

## Applications

Projecting illuminated lilac sign; shopfront in a Victorian brick building (coral window with shape cut-out and trace line, mint window with a giant cropped molar and booking line, plum arch on the glazed door, lilac vinyl headline on the fanlight); billboard and bus shelter launch posters; reception with coral curved desk, red arch niche for retail, coral column, coral and teal lounge chairs, timber; wall wayfinding ("This way for pearly whites"); shape door signs; framed posters; shelf signs; tote bag; website (mint hero, dentist in shape window); Instagram grid; appointment confirmation screens; animated type sequences.

What varies: hue family, which of the four shapes, how it crops, whether it holds a photo, a line trace or nothing; the pun. What stays fixed: GT Pressura Extended in sentence case, tonal same-hue type, the arch logo placed small in a corner, flat grounds with one shape, real smiling faces.

## What makes it ownable

The arch-with-mono-wordmark lockup; the family of four grid-built tooth shapes, especially the molar with its two lobes and notch; tonal lilac-and-plum as the lead colour pair in a white-and-blue category; the pun headlines in wide GT Pressura Extended. Any one of these alone (a lilac panel with a plum sentence and a cropped soft tooth) is recognisable without the logo.

## What a generic version would have been

A white, pale-blue and navy practice with a tooth icon carrying a sparkle or a smile-curve swoosh under a clean sans wordmark; stock photography of a dentist in a mask with a mirror and a patient in a chair; headlines like "Excellence in dental care" or "Your smile, our passion"; a minimal white waiting room with chrome and grey seating.

## Principles it demonstrates

1. Derive the graphic shapes from the product's own anatomy, then abstract them on a strict construction grid until they read as friendly forms first and literal objects second.
2. Make the brand shape work as a container (a window for photos, words, doors and shelves) so a single device carries the whole system.
3. In a category defined by a colour code, pick a palette from a different world and use it tonally, each layout in one hue with type in its darkest shade.
4. Let the logo be one member of the device family, so the mark and the system are visibly the same idea.
5. Carry the device into three dimensions (door panels, niches, desks, signs), so the space itself becomes the identity.
6. Replace the category's fear vocabulary with light, confident puns about the outcome the customer wants.
