# Writing the brief

Brief the character the way an art director briefs an illustrator: who, attitude, the face, the
thing it's doing, the hand. Short and concrete. Say what the character is like and how its face
looks, and let the drawing find the body's shapes; judge the result by eye.

## What the brief says

- **Who it is**, as behaviour rather than an adjective: what it's doing and feeling right now.
- **The one thing** that makes it itself: a feature, a habit, a prop it's never without. Without it,
  every candidate is the category's mascot.
- **What makes it this brand's**: the detail from the business's own world it wears, holds or does.
- **Its face**, from its attitude: the lids, the brows, where it looks, the mouth, in three to five
  concrete terms, with one thing uneven. The face is where a character's personality reads, at 32
  px as on a poster.
- **Its pose**: one gesture with the business's own object, smaller than the character.
- **What it leaves out**: only what the brief or the category's codes rule out.

Not in the brief: a count of limbs, where each one goes, a colour per part, a list of bans against
clichés. That is an assembly diagram, and the model builds a thing standing still. Colours go in the
prompt as the brand's palette with a job each (`styles.md`).

## The face

The words that change a face, a few at a time:
- **lids**: heavy, half-closed, wide open, one lower than the other;
- **brows**: one higher, knitted, shot up, flat and unimpressed;
- **where it looks**: at you, off to one side, up at something, down its nose;
- **the eyes themselves**: small pupils in big whites, big dark pupils, one eye bigger;
- **the mouth**: lopsided, a small O, pressed shut, wide open laughing, a gap in the teeth.

The face follows the attitude, so a sleepy character and a proud one get different faces. Generic
words ("a cheeky grin", "happy eyes", "a big smile") read as no instruction, and the model
fills them with its stock face: two closed crescents and a smile.

## Two briefs for the same character

For Dew, the made-up mascot of Lumen, the example skincare brand:

Weak:
> A round water drop, wider at the bottom, its point curling at the top; two happy closed eyes and a
> cheerful smile; two short arms ending in round mitts, one raised.

Strong:
> Dew is a sleepy water drop who loves slow mornings: leaning back mid-stretch, one little arm
> raised in a yawn. Heavy half-closed lids, one eye cracked open to check the time, brows loose and
> uneven, its mouth a lopsided yawning O. Its point curls over at the top like a bed-head curl. It
> is the drop of serum Lumen puts on your skin, and it never hurries.

The weak brief gets a drop with sticks for arms and the stock face. The strong one says what Dew is
like and how its face shows it, and leaves the body's shapes to the illustrator.

## Three ideas for one character

The three candidates are the same character in three briefs that each change the idea. For Dew,
beside the sleepy one:

> Dew, proud of a job done: up on tiptoe, chest out, one arm presenting a tiny Lumen dropper like a
> trophy. Eyes bright and turned to you, one brow arched, a closed smile pushed up on one side, its
> curl standing straight up.

> Dew, startled awake: mid-hop, both arms flung out, its curl sprung upright. Eyes wide with small
> pupils, brows shot up, its mouth a small o pulled off to one side.

The name, what it is, its build, the look and the palette stay; the attitude, the face and the
pose change.

## When a drawing misses

Change the brief, never describe the failed drawing back to the model, and draw again. Once a
drawing is kept, it is the anchor and then the sheet: from then on the identity lives in images, and
the words say who it is, how its face is made and that nothing about it changes (`poses.md`).
