# Fjord — floating sauna and cold plunge, Sausalito (San Francisco Bay)

- **Name / business / place / year / studio:** Fjord, a floating sauna and bay-water cold plunge moored at 2310 Marinship Way, Sausalito, on Richardson Bay (San Francisco Bay). Founders Alex Yenni and Gabe Turner; architecture by Nick Polansky; photography by Alex Farnum. Identity, logo and bespoke font by DutchScot (London, Greenwich Design District), bespoke display face made with Playtype (Copenhagen). Venue opened around July 2025 (Dwell covered it as newly open that month); DutchScot published the case in June 2026, BP&O reviewed it 16 July 2026, Fonts In Use listed it 23 Sept 2026. So the identity dates from 2025 (launch with the venue, inferred) and entered the design press in 2026. Not to be confused with Fjord Sauna in Flåm, Norway (fjordsauna.com), a separate business.

## Brief & business truth
Two wood-lined Finnish saunas (up to six people each, heated to about 190°F) on a salvaged concrete breakwater barge left from the 2013 America's Cup, built partly from repurposed shipping containers, with guests plunging straight into the bay at roughly 50–55°F. Shared 90-minute sessions at about the price of a yoga class, private sessions and buyouts; reservations drop monthly and sell out. The founders explicitly reject the spa and "biohacker" framings: it is communal, outdoors, self-guided and ends with a drink with friends. DutchScot's own brief line is essentially "Fjord is not a spa; it does not sell spa services." The identity had to sell a raw physical experience to Bay Area locals without looking like a wellness brand, and belong to Sausalito's working waterfront (Marinship was a WWII shipyard).

## Strategy / insight
Position Fjord between architecture, hospitality and outdoor recreation rather than wellness: a social, self-directed encounter with the landscape and with the physiological extremes of heat and cold. Sidestep spa tropes by borrowing from the area's industrial past (dockside infrastructure, utilitarian signage), and let temperature contrast, the single defining fact of the product, drive every formal decision without ever illustrating it literally.

## The idea (one sentence)
The degree sign replaces the letter O, so every word the brand sets (starting with its own name, FJ°RD) carries a temperature reading inside it.

**Its source:** moment (the switch between 190° and 55°, captured by the unit of measurement) combined with reversal of category codes (industrial dock signage instead of spa softness).

## Register
Industrial strict minimal: black and white slabs, one heavy squared display face, a neutral grotesque, a single dark accent, and full-bleed documentary photography. Minimal in vocabulary but loud in scale, closer to dock stencilling and gym posters than to Scandinavian calm.

## Category codes it used or broke
- **Broke:** spa pastels, sage/sand palettes, thin elegant serifs, lowercase softness, water droplets and leaf icons, "relax / restore / glow" language, serene stock imagery of candles and stones. Replaced with all-caps slab-like grotesque, pure black, commands in the imperative, cold water and bodies in motion.
- **Broke:** Nordic cliché. Despite the name, no runes, no Viking motifs, no fjord landscapes, no blue-and-white hygge (BP&O notes the absence of Scandinavian clichés).
- **Used, re-coded:** the thermometer and the temperature number, the most clinical code of the sauna category, promoted from information to brand voice (posters are nothing but 189F° and 55F°).
- **Used:** wood and water appear only through photography of the real place, never as graphic motifs.

## Mark
- **Primary logo:** the wordmark FJ°RD set in Exit Fjord, white on black (or black on white). The guidelines state it was crafted specifically for Fjord, inspired by Sausalito's industrial past, and must always be used from the supplied artwork.
- **Symbol (shorthand logo):** "F °". The wordmark with J, R and D erased, leaving the capital F and the degree glyph in their original positions, so a full letter-width gap remains between them. It reads simultaneously as the brand's initial and as a Fahrenheit reading written back to front (F then degree), which is the private joke of the system (the Fahrenheit reading is inferred). Guidelines assign it to small and square uses such as social avatars; it also heads wayfinding signs. A small woven brand label appears on the felt sauna hat (content too small to read in the published image).
- **Construction of the degree glyph:** a small rounded square ring with a square counter (not a circle), roughly 40% of cap height, its top aligned to cap height so it hangs in the upper part of the line with empty space below it. Stroke weight is close to the capitals' stems, so it reads as a solid object, not a superscript. The same glyph, scaled up to fill a poster, becomes a monumental rounded-square frame (poster application), and scaled down becomes the pixel unit of the patterns.
- **Variants seen:** horizontal wordmark (primary), F° symbol, wordmark reversed out of full-bleed photography, huge cropped wordmark bleeding off the web hero. No stacked lockup or descriptor lockup was published.

## Wordmark / lettering
Bespoke version of Exit (Playtype, Jonas Hecksher, 2012), a single-weight, very heavy, squared grotesque derived from French hand-painted sign lettering of around 1900, which looks monospaced without being so. Caps only. Squared bowls with small inner radii, flat terminals, tight but even spacing. The modification: the O is replaced by the degree glyph described above. The numeral zero keeps its normal full-height rounded-rectangle form, so "0" and "°" remain distinct. Everything else is stock Exit, which is why the change registers: it is the only anomaly in an otherwise uniform wall of capitals.

## Type system
- **Exit Fjord** (the bespoke font, file name ExitFjord-Regular): headlines, large messaging, posters, signage, packaging, merch, Instagram stories, web hero. Always uppercase; set very large with tight leading (lines nearly touching), often flush-left stacks of three short lines. The O-swap applies to every word, so any headline becomes branded: SL°WLY THEN ALL AT °NCE, GET INT° Y°UR B°DY, THE °NLY WAY °UT IS THR°UGH, FEEL S°METHING, SAUSALIT°. Guidelines limit it to bigger headings and headlines.
- **Exit (regular, unmodified)** is also loaded on the website alongside Exit Fjord (per site CSS), presumably where a true O is needed (inferred).
- **Saans** (Displaay, Prague): all running copy, captions, addresses, labels, and uppercase tracked-out wayfinding text (NO ENTRY WITHOUT A RESERVATION / PLEASE CHECK IN AT THE KIOSK). Its O stays a normal O. Used small and quiet, sentence case in body, generous tracking when set in caps on signs.
- **Type as image:** giant numerals as the whole poster (189F °, 55F °, F °, each right-aligned so the degree glyphs stack in one column at the right edge like a gauge scale); a single ° enlarged to fill an A-format poster; SAU / NAL / IT° broken across three lines as a block; oversized wordmark cropped by the page edge on the web hero; website brochure paragraph in Saans with key nouns (FLOATING SAUNA, SAN FRANCISCO BAY, NORDIC) set in capitals within the sentence.
- **Temperature numerals:** format is number + F + wide gap + degree glyph (e.g. 189F °, 55F °), the unit order reversed from convention so the pair F° always echoes the symbol. The website's editorial copy uses the conventional 190°F.

## Colour system
Published in the guidelines (colour page):
- **Black** #000000 (R0 G0 B0): dominant ground, largest share (roughly half in the swatch proportions).
- **White** #FFFFFF (R255 G255 B255): type and reversed grounds, second share (about 40%).
- **Burgundy** #511011 (R81 G16 B17): accent, smallest share (a narrow sliver, around 10%). The live website uses a close burgundy, #541414, in its CSS.
Roles: black/white carry the cold, hard, industrial register; burgundy is the only warmth, read by BP&O as heated timber or flushed skin. In the applications shown, burgundy is used very sparingly; nearly everything is black and white plus the colour of the photography and of kraft board (inferred from the published images). No tints, gradients or secondary palette. Allowed pairs: white on black (default), black on white, white on photography, white print on kraft (soap box). Forbidden uses were not published.

## Device(s) / graphic language
- **Two particle patterns (hot and cold):** fields of the degree glyph used as dots. **55°** (cold water): a dense, strictly regular half-drop grid (alternate rows offset by half a step, like a close-packed lattice). **189°** (steam): the same glyph scattered sparsely and irregularly, as if molecules had expanded into gas. They illustrate phase change without drawing water or steam. Used as an additional graphic asset, e.g. overlaid in white on photography in Instagram stories; each pattern is labelled with its temperature in Exit Fjord.
- **Temperature gauge on the website:** a vertical gauge moves up and down as the user scrolls, and the left-hand navigation is ruled like the markings on a thermometer.
- **Black panel with photo windows:** posters are black fields with rectangular, unframed photographs set into the grid, type overlapping the photo edges; small Saans lines (Sauna. Plunge. Wild swimming / the street address) sit flush right as captions.
- **Edge cropping:** wordmark and giant glyphs are allowed to bleed off the format; type is huge relative to the frame.
- **Coordinates:** the site's latitude/longitude set in Exit Fjord with ° (37.8591 °N, 122.4853 °W) as a place signature, turning the degree into a geographic mark as well as a thermal one.

## Imagery
Photography by Alex Farnum: documentary, real guests and the real vessel, never staged spa still life. Subjects: silhouettes on the deck at sunrise/sunset, swimmers mid-stroke or floating, splashes, bathers climbing the ladder, water pouring onto stove stones, the cedar-lined sauna with its full-width bay window, bodies in towels facing the water. Light: low sun, backlight, strong contrast, deep shadow; some images converted to grainy black and white with fog. Crop: tight and physical, often cutting figures; water surface fills frames. Colour grade: natural, slightly cool water tones against warm wood. Absent: candles, towels folded into swans, massage, products, smiling-at-camera portraits, leaves, droplets on glass.

## Voice
Short, physical, imperative, a little dare-like, and unsentimental. Headline lines published: FEEL SOMETHING (the site title and main line), GET INTO YOUR BODY, SLOWLY THEN ALL AT ONCE, THE ONLY WAY OUT IS THROUGH. Body copy is warmer and plain: an escape from climate-controlled comfort zones and uninspired routines; heat as medicine; the Nordic cycle of hot and cold; "Sauna. Plunge. Wild swimming." as a three-word descriptor. Rules evident: speak about sensation and body, not relaxation or treatments; no spa vocabulary (pamper, indulge, treatment, ritual); signs are blunt and polite in plain caps (NO ENTRY WITHOUT A RESERVATION, THANK YOU). The degree swap turns every word with an O into a pun, so copy tends to favour O-rich lines (inferred).

## Applications
Hanging dock sign (black panel, white wordmark, among timber slats); wayfinding and rules signs (F° symbol plus Saans caps); posters (temperature numerals, giant °, SAU/NAL/IT°); soap gift token in kraft box with white print, sold instead of a plastic gift card or email voucher (a handmade sandalwood and bergamot bar on a rope); felt sauna hat with woven F° label; website on Squarespace with scroll-driven temperature gauge and full-bleed wordmark hero (desktop and mobile); Instagram stories with headlines and particle patterns over photography; brochure/about paragraph.
**What varies:** scale (from a tiny F° on a sign to a poster-filling °), ground (black, white, photo, kraft), which temperature is quoted, the headline line, cold vs steam pattern. **What stays fixed:** Exit Fjord caps with the ° swap, black/white base, Saans for all small text, flush-right small captions, documentary photography.

## Guidelines structure
Style guide (shared as pages on the DutchScot case study): cover/wordmark over photo; brand statement paragraph; headline examples over photography; Logo (usage note: use supplied artwork); Symbol (shorthand F°, social avatars); temperature-numeral lockups; Typography: Exit Fjord (headlines only); Typography: Saans (longer copy); Colour (Black, White, Burgundy with hex and RGB); Pattern (55° cold / 189° steam); location/coordinates slide; full Exit Fjord character set.

## What makes it ownable
- The degree-sign O: one glyph swap that brands every word, recognisable in any headline even without the logo.
- The F° symbol as initial plus reversed Fahrenheit reading.
- Temperature numbers (189F °, 55F °) used as the entire message.
- The ° glyph reused as the pixel of the hot and cold particle patterns.
- Pure black and white with a single dark burgundy #511011.

## What a generic version would have been
A lowercase thin serif or rounded sans "fjord" wordmark, maybe with a wave or mountain line icon; sage green, sand and misty blue palette; soft-focus photos of steam, stones and eucalyptus; taglines about restoring, unwinding and self-care rituals; Nordic motifs (runes, pine trees, fjord silhouettes) to justify the name; a spa-style gift card. Fjord replaced all of that with dock-sign capitals, black, numbers and cold water.

## Principles it demonstrates
1. Find the product's unit of measure and put it inside the letterforms, so the idea travels with every word rather than living only in the logo.
2. Make exactly one modification to an otherwise stock typeface; a single anomaly in a uniform system is more ownable than many.
3. Derive the symbol by subtraction from the wordmark, keeping the original spacing, so the shorthand still reads as the name.
4. Reuse the signature glyph as the building unit of the graphic devices, so patterns carry the same meaning as the type.
5. When a category is soft, borrow the visual codes of the place's working history and let the photography carry the sensuality.
6. Express the brand's core contrast in the palette's proportions: an overwhelmingly cold monochrome with one small warm accent.
