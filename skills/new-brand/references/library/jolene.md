# Jolene

- **Name:** Jolene
- **Business:** Bakery and restaurant (bread, pastry, all-day dining, e-commerce shop); now several sites across north London
- **Place:** Newington Green, London, UK
- **Year:** 2018 (logotype, opening Sept 2018; press 2019); extension 2025 (J-marques, animations, website/e-commerce, photography)
- **Studio:** Studio Frith (founder Frith Kerr; design director Claire Köster). Photography: Clare Shilland, James Hole.

## Brief & business truth

Third venue from restaurateur Jeremie Cometto-Lingenheim and chef David Gingell (after Primeur and Westerns Laundry). The business is built on regenerative wheat (Wildfarmed grain, own milling), a circular economy and the idea that bread should nourish the future: buying a loaf supports soil regeneration. The founder asked for a logo that looked naive, almost childish, to carry that future-facing ethos. The space itself is rough plaster walls, bentwood chairs, zinc/steel tables, chalked bookings. Problem solved: signal a serious, ethics-first bakery without any of the category's artisan clichés, and with a mark that would not age.

## Strategy / insight

Children stand for the future; a bakery whose whole mission is the soil for the next generation should literally be signed by the next generation. Kerr translated "naive" into "hope" rather than "cute". In 2025 the studio's operating principle was to "unfilter" themselves and treat play as the structure, acting as custodians of an already-loved brand rather than redesigning it.

## The idea

**One sentence:** The name is written by a six-year-old, and that real child's hand (not an imitation of it) is the whole identity.
**Source:** people (the next generation, literally a child) tied to the business's purpose (regeneration for the future).

## Register

Naive / handmade-real, within an otherwise restrained, quiet, gallery-like system. Low-key, not loud.

## Category codes it used or broke

- Broke: faux-rustic letterpress, sepia nostalgia, wheat sheaves, script logos, kraft-and-stamp "artisan" kit.
- Broke: typographic polish as a sign of quality. Quality is signalled by restraint around the mark (plain sans, lots of space, good photography), not by the mark itself.
- Used: the bakery's tactile materials (cotton bread bags, paper carriers, curtains), but as blank grounds for the handwriting rather than as decoration.

## Mark

- **Type:** hand-lettered wordmark (genuine child's handwriting, selected from several attempts), plus a family of "J-marques" added in 2025.
- **Construction:** single monoline pencil stroke, no contrast. Letters read J-o-l-E-N-E: a capital J with a hooked tail, then a small lowercase o and a short lowercase l, then three capitals E, N, E that grow progressively larger. The baseline climbs to the right (roughly 8-10 degrees, inferred from photos) and the final E is about twice the height of the o: Kerr chose this version for its spacing and the way the letters build to a crescendo. Spacing is irregular and was not corrected. Stroke pressure varies (letters get darker as they grow, per Köster).
- **Optical details:** deliberately not redrawn, smoothed or vectorised into regularity; imperfections present in the drawing were kept in every digitisation.
- **How it is treated:** the studio stopped treating it as a logotype and started treating it as an image; once it was a picture it could be placed loosely, rotated slightly, stamped off-register, embroidered or printed without breaking rules.
- **Variants:** J-marques (2025): a child was asked to draw faces and scenes around the J taken from the logotype. Results include a J as nose-and-brow with dot eyes, a J as the outline of a face with lashes and green lips, a J-house, a J on a skateboard. Heavy black brush-marker line, occasionally one flat fill colour. Animated versions keep hand-drawn frame rhythm (not tweened smoothness).
- **Small size:** the J-marque serves as the compact mark (favicon/merch) (inferred from towel, hat and web usage).

## Wordmark / lettering

Custom (child's hand). Mixed case by accident, kept on purpose. No tracking logic; the unevenness is the identity. Never retyped in a font.

## Type system

- **Karla** (grotesque sans, Jonathan Pinhorn): primary; menus, web, e-commerce, information. Chosen because it is plain, free and universal, so it never competes with the handwriting.
- **Alda** (Berton Hasebe): secondary; a sharper, warmer serif for accent and editorial moments.
- Rule (inferred): the handwriting carries all personality; set type stays small, neutral and orderly. No second display face.

## Colour system

No published palette. Observed (inferred): the mark in a single dark ink (black or dark brown) on bag colours that change by product: saffron yellow, cocoa brown, washed pink cotton bread bags; terracotta and cream grounds for J-marques; the dusty pink of the shopfront and interior; flat accents (leaf green) only inside illustrations. Colour lives in materials and objects more than in a strict brand palette.

## Device(s) / graphic language

- **Children's drawings as collage:** children draw freely; designers then select and collage, "Dadaist" in Köster's words. Rule: don't over-direct the child, don't over-polish the result.
- **J-marque characters:** the J is the constant; what grows around it varies (face, house, skateboarder, flowers and rain clouds on a tote).
- **Play within parameters:** the e-commerce site is structurally clear; play happens at chosen moments (changing homepage animation, a daily photo slot for staff, a surprise at checkout).

## Imagery

Film-like, natural-light photography by Clare Shilland and James Hole: loaves standing in cotton bags against white, the corner shopfront in sun, dogs by a hanging tote, staff and friends in merch, hand-made ceramics. Warm, candid, a little art-world; described as warmth plus sharp chic. Never: flour-dusted rustic tabletops, sepia grade, stock food styling.

## Voice

Minimal published copy guidance. Behaviour: quiet, few words, lets objects speak; playful moments are visual (a letter sent off on a skateboard to buy bread, thrown kisses) rather than verbal jokes (inferred).

## Applications

Cotton bread bags in several colours, paper carrier bags, canvas totes, shop fascia (hand-painted-looking wordmark), embroidered window curtains, loosely stamped daily menus, chalk booking boards, website and e-commerce shop, animated J-marques, towels, T-shirts, caps, collaboration ceramics.
**What varies:** material, colour of the ground, scale, placement, the drawing around the J. **What stays fixed:** the same single handwriting artwork, one dark ink, plain sans for information.

## Guidelines structure

None published. The studio describes itself as the brand's custodian rather than issuing a rulebook.

## What makes it ownable

The specific handwriting (mixed case, rising crescendo, uneven spacing) is impossible to recreate with a font; the J-with-a-face family extends it; the gap between a child's scrawl and a serious, restrained setting.

## What a generic version would have been

A letterpress-style serif or brush script, a wheat-ear or rolling-pin icon, kraft paper with a rubber stamp, a cream-and-brown palette, the words "artisan" and "est. 2018".

## Principles it demonstrates

1. Borrow a real hand, not an imitation of one: authenticity comes from where a mark was made, not from its style.
2. When a mark is irregular, treat it as an image with its own simple rules instead of forcing typographic rules on it.
3. Put one loud element in a quiet system: make everything around an expressive mark plain so the contrast carries the message.
4. Extend an identity by taking one letter from the mark and letting it become characters, not by adding new symbols.
5. Tie the mark's making process to the business's purpose so the story is true, not decoration.
